*Hell is the fire light at the end of the tunnel, heaven is the darkness. We die and are drawn to it. Our souls are eaten like moths by a cosmic spider.*

Pastor Matt was telling another sermon about the light of God. The church was full this early in the morning, the morning sunlight dim from the single skylight high overhead. I struggled to stay awake while a message that I'd heard a hundred times in different forms emanated from the pulpit.

"Then God said, 'Let there be light, and there was light."

I picked up one of the pencils from the holder in the back of the pew and idly spun it. First in my right hand, then tried my left. The non-dominant hand was clumsier and the pencil dropped to the maroon carpet with a muffled clatter. The sound barely registered among the background noises of people shifting in the hard wooden pews, young children coloring, and the occasional cough. The pastor kept speaking.

"I am the light of the world. Whoever follows me will never walk in darkness, but will have the light of life."

I watched Matt speaking for a while. He was a younger man with eyes that seemed to shine, a replacement for our last pastor after some kind of church drama that I didn't care enough to follow. I'd vaguely heard something about the last pastor preaching heresy, but who cared about heresy in this day and age? That word was for fiction or Catholics, and who knew what it actually meant for us now. He'd probably just pissed off someone higher up who invented a reason to pry him out of his cozy little congregation. 

I didn't have any problems with Matt personally, he'd been very professional during my mom's funeral. Didn't like him or his services enough to stick around, but I didn't plan to stay in the town very long anyway.

The light fixture next to the lectern was very bright, with a shade made of frosted glass. I could see a large number of insect and moth corpses collected in the base of the glass. A disgusting amount, how long had it been since it had been cleaned?

Matt's tone of voice was building in volume, an indication that he was about to finish. Thank God.

"The people dwelling in darkness have seen a great light, and for those dwelling in the region and shadow of death, on them a light has dawned.

Do not be afraid of the light, embrace it, cherish it, and feel its life-giving warmth! God's love radiates from it, and we only have to walk forward into it to feel his divine embrace!"

He paused, and smiled at all of us,

"My brothers and sisters in Christ, don't be afraid of going into the light!"

A few polite chuckles bubbled up at the awkward attempt at humor, and it was finally over. A row over, an enthusiastic "Amen!" came from Mr Davis and his wife. The benediction was given and we all filed out into the foyer.

I saw Mr Davis standing alone and went over to speak to him, and get some coffee from the ancient machine in the corner. The Davis's were both long-standing and well-respected members of the congregation. He was a brilliant man who had worked at some government agency as a programmer a long time ago, and now kept birds and tutored mathematics. He'd taught me trigonometry and calculus throughout high school, and I hadn't seen him since I'd moved away. He expressed condolences and I thanked him.

His eyes seemed brighter than I remembered, and as we talked I developed a headache behind my eyes. I poured myself another cup of coffee and asked how he'd been. He talked about his recent health problems.

The doctors weren't sure exactly what had precipitated it, but he had simply fallen at home and hit his head. His wife found him and called 911, and while in the hospital there were rapid complications that left him with multi-organ failure. He was clinically dead for a few minutes before he was revived. Then he died a second time and was brought back. They were concerned that he'd suffered brain damage, but he bounced back rapidly and made an almost full recovery.

I smiled and expressed my congratulations at his recovery, and he nodded. Then I stopped smiling. He told me that he had gone to heaven and seen God those few minutes he was dead. He said that he had seen a bright light appear above him and was drawn into it. While was was in heaven he could feel the warmth of God around him, and even feel his holy touch pulling him closer. How darkness surrounded him then and he was back on earth. And how he still remembers that light and longs to return to it.

I nodded politely in confusion. This was very uncharacteristic of him. He'd always been on the agnostic side. Both he and his wife came here more for the community, not the theology. I'd never speak it out loud, but maybe he'd sustained brain damage after all. My headache was building the longer I spoke to him, and the coffee wasn't helping.

His wife evidently finished some business in the office and came over to join us. She was equally brilliant but applied herself to the church office, taking care of bookkeeping, organization, and the church funds. 

She chimed in when she heard him talking about his hospital stay. She spoke about how his health problems had been a blessing in disguise. He was a different man, changed for the better. Whatever had happened to him while he was on death's door had turned him into a different person, and his enthusiasm and fervor for life had lit up all those around him. The light of God's love had illuminated both their lives and shown them the path of righteousness.

I nodded politely again and made congratulatory noises. I hardly recognized either of them. Maybe his change could be explained by his health problems, but the way that the reserved woman I remembered was now gushing about the light of God was strange. I was happy that they got some positivity out of the experience, but I was beginning to realize that I wasn't just having a headache. This was turning into a migraine. I hadn't had one since middle school but I remembered the pain, nausea, and flashes of blinding light very well.

I excused myself. Mrs Davis offered me an Advil from her purse, which I accepted. Mr Davis told me he'd be in touch and that he had some textbooks that he didn't need anymore if I wanted to come by and pick them up. Barely paying attention, I agreed and walked out to the parking lot. 

At home I took another Advil and closed all the curtains, then laid down. Even dim light made my eyes ache, flashes of lightning spider-webbed across my vision with every pulse of pain, and my stomach felt like it was being turned inside out. I puked into a trash can, and the force of my stomach contracting made my vision shatter into shards of scintillating glass and my head explode in agony.

I laid there for what felt like eternity. My phone rang, and the noise and light from the little screen were such an assault on my haywire senses that I threw the damn thing out of the room. Finally I fell asleep next to the trash can, the pain fading into a dull ache.

In my dream I was finally in blessed darkness. It folded around me and soothed my head. I lay in a comfortable space outside of reality and time, trying not to feel anything at all. My brain felt fragile, like any sudden movement would cause it to fall into pieces. I needed the rest, time to recuperate.

I gradually began to hear a sound like footsteps in the darkness. They came closer, and I turned towards the noise, but the darkness was absolute. I tried to speak but no words came out. But I could hear faint words in the darkness around me, that voice is familiar?

"Don't go... don't go... don't go... the light isn't..."

Then the pastor's voice spoke in my mind,

"Then God said, 'Let there be light, and there was light."

And the dream disappeared in a blinding flash that dragged me into wakefulness. It was late afternoon, and somehow the blinds had fallen off one of the windows. Maybe I'd been too violent trying to close it when I got home and dislodged it. The sun was positioned to shine directly into my room, although now it didn't cause me such pain. I looked at the bottle of ginger ale and bin full of puke, and spit to clear my mouth. Then went to wash my face.

In the hallway, I found my phone. My throw had broken the screen, and a bright spider-web of cracks had spread across it. Luckily it still worked. A missed call from Davis was there. A text from him listed out the textbooks he had for me to pick up. There were a few biology and ecology books I had no idea what do do with, but the calculus ones were recent enough for resale. I still felt a little sick, but decided that it was worthwhile. And maybe getting out of the house would keep me from focusing on the feeling. I called to setup a time with him.

I arrived at their house that evening. I had thought that they went to bed on the early side, but the house was ablaze with light. Even the yard was full of new light fixtures that looked recently installed. The house glowed from within with every color of bulb. Moths, mayflies, and other bugs swarmed around the fixtures and windows in the dark, and their tiny chitinous bodies littered the ground around the lights.

I picked my way down the long driveway, feeling uncomfortable. As I got closer to the house, I noticed the smell of something rotting. Maybe a squirrel or something had died in the bushes. I knocked on the door, looking back toward my car. The light around me was so glaring that it was difficult to see it parked on the unlit road.

The door opened and Davis stood in the entryway, but I wasn't paying any attention to him. When the door had opened, a thick wave of the rotting stench had rolled out over me. My eyes stung, stomach lurched, and I gagged. Through tears I looked past him in curiosity and disgust. The bodies of the birds he used to keep were littering the floor in various states of decay. A few huddled on high perches, looking down. 

I was struggling with concern, pity, disgust, fear, and confusion. As I stood there in the entryway, some of the insects around the outdoor lights found their way inside, buzzing around and casting tiny flickering shadows that were instantly snuffed out. I realized that Mr Davis was saying something,

"So sorry that our home is such a mess, we haven't had much time to clean lately I'm afraid. It's been so busy up at the church since Pastor Matthew came.

If you're in a hurry, I can just bring the books to you."

He turned towards the interior, his bare foot coming down on a tiny rotting ribcage with a wet crunch. Back in the kitchen, I could hear a radio sermon playing,

"The eye is the lamp of the body. So, if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light, but if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness. If then the light in you is darkness, how great is the darkness!"

The remains of the migraine from earlier stirred behind my eyes, set off by the glaring illumination searing into my skull from all angles. This was a mistake. I didn't need these books and the smell wasn't helping either. This charnel house occupied the same location that I spent so many afternoons at as a teenager, but it was unrecognizable in this new light. Davis yelled something that I couldn't make out and then returned with a few books, his feet slick with black blood and feathers.

"There's one more stack, you can take these and I'll help you bring the rest out."

Without really knowing what I was doing, I started walking back to the car, propelled by nausea and my returning migraine.

Davis came up behind me, carrying a few more textbooks. The sweet smell of rot wasn't as bad back in the yard but it still filled the air, drowning all else out.

"It's such beautiful weather we're having. I try to appreciate all I can nowadays. Life is such a precious gift, and my trip to the other side made me see so many things I didn't see before. It's easy not to notice these things, but even the air smells good on nights like these!"

My stomach churned. With the beat of my heart flashes of lightning spider-webbed across my vision, accompanied by stabbing pain in my eye sockets.

We arrived at the car and stacked all the books in the trunk. I didn't care anymore, I just wanted to get away but didn't want to offend someone I'd once respected by taking off prematurely. Maybe I'd contact somebody tomorrow to check on him or ask his wife if they needed help. I said goodbye and quickly drove off.

The drive was normally a nice one down a mountain road, but in the dark with a migraine it was difficult. Luckily there weren't any other cars in the road. A tunnel loomed ahead.
