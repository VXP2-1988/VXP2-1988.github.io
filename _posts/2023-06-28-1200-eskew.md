Spoilers ahead.

I Am in Eskew is a single-narrator podcast that follows David Ward, a broken man uncertainly navigating daily life in a hostile city named Eskew. The podcast spans 31 episodes, each episode running 25-45 minutes. Most episodes have a single self-contained story that relates back to an overall theme of David gaining self awareness and losing hope as he attempts to escape the city. 

Midway through the podcast a second narrator is introduced, a woman named Riyu who has been hired to find David. She grounds the story in the real world and provides shadowy clues to what Eskew is and the various factions that are attempting to destroy it or harness its power. Her practicality and resolve is a counterpoint to David's sentimentality and despair. 

Eskew is a mix of horror and weird fiction, with a little bit of deadpan, dark humor thrown in. David's narration is mostly monotone and doesn't really spell much out or hold the listener's hand. It covers themes like obsession and body horror which are very Junji Ito; alienation, absurdity, and bizarre bureaucracy is very Kafka; and the way the city warps space, time, and sanity is straight up Lovecraft. There's probably other influences I'm missing but there's a rich and unique flavor, especially for a first time amateur production.

Many of the other episodes have social themes that are pretty smartly done. Often the writers will take an event like homelessness, bullying, conflict with coworkers, immigration, losing touch with a loved one, or loneliness and put their characters through it. Somehow they succeed in creating a unique feeling of horror, hopelessness, and empathy while highlighting how close our world is to the nightmare of Eskew. Very little about the city or its inhabitants is truly alien, it's just thousands of people put into an environment that exacerbates all their worst impulses. The entire city "loves pain as long as someone else is feeling it" and David isn't always an exception.

The city itself is essentially a living organism. It grows and has a will of its own, assigning constantly changing roles to its inhabitants that they are forced to act out in order to somehow feed the city. It's somehow both modern and unreasonably old, and writes its own history as it sees fit. The city "wants to be observed" and needs an audience to witness its violence. 

It's unclear whether the city is something otherworldly or manmade; the term "hostile architecture" is used frequently to describe it. In an incomplete excerpt, we're told that an organization was experimenting with a way to destabilize large population centers and destroy life without weapons or warfare. Their solution was to design streets, doors, and buildings in a seemingly benign way that would have devastating long term psychological effects. The experiment was abandoned because anyone who interacted with the test site never returned. However, there's also evidence that people were getting backrooms'd before the test site and that maybe whatever dimension was eating them simply used the test to gain form and begin to spread.

The story ends with the city making a hostile incursion into the real world as a new property development. The ending is the only weak part of the writing, since it's an anticlimax. The city essentially runs low on resources entering reality and abandons David and Riyu. David opts for a kind of conscious death in the darkness between reality and the city, and Riyu's fate is ambiguous as she takes a city cab away.

It was still a great series, 4/5 stars. Transcripts here: https://www.iamineskew.com/transcripts
